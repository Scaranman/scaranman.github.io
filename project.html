Guest Pass

Temporary door access for LenelS2's Elements platform. Hosts create a pass, share a link, and guests unlock doors from their phone before credentials expire automatically.

MY ROLE UX Designer (co-op), end-to-end
TIMELINE Jun – Dec 2021 · shipped Q4 2021
THE TEAM Product, engineering, stakeholders

Project overview

The challenge

Before Guest Pass, granting visitor access in Elements required creating a full person record, normally reserved for employees, and manually revoking it later. Customers needed a way to grant temporary visitors access without creating permanent credentials or coordinating access through the lobby.

The solution

Hosts create a pass by setting start and end times, assigning door readers, and sharing a link. Guests open the pass on their phone and tap to unlock. When the access window closes, the pass is deleted automatically with no lingering credentials.

Context

LenelS2 develops physical security software for access control, video, and mobile credentials. Elements is their cloud platform for managing badge readers and cameras across a facility.

KEY IMPACT Q4 2021

Released in Q4 2021. The feature is included in sales demonstrations and has generated ongoing requests for additional capability.

User groups served 2 (host + guest)
Records created per guest 0 (was 1)
Post-launch feature requests Numerous

What the feature had to do

Hosts and guests do different jobs on the same pass, and security teams need an event log when someone actually uses it.

ISSUING A PASS
  • Hosts issue and send passes themselves and remain responsible for the guest
  • Optional start and end date
  • Assign specific door readers
  • A way to share the pass, plus an email template
  • The ability to update a pass after sending
USING A PASS
  • Guests receive the pass by email with a link to a web UI
  • The UI shows the validity window and who issued it
  • One unlock button per assigned reader
EVENTS
  • The Elements event feed records when a pass is used and at which reader

From a low-fi flow to the shipped UI

Flow first in InVision Freehand, then hi-fi in Figma using the LenelS2 design system I helped maintain.

Figure 1.1 — InVision Freehand flow: create, share, use, and manage a guest pass.

1. Guest pass list A dedicated area of Elements where hosts see active, scheduled, and expired passes.
2. Create a pass Name, activation and expiration, then readers. In hi-fi I replaced the recipient field with a share step.
Design decision

Sharing became its own step

In the low-fidelity flow, the recipient email lived inside the create form, which meant any edit risked re-sending the pass. I moved sharing to its own screen so hosts can copy the link through any channel, and edits no longer notify the guest.

Animation as feedback

Guest passes live on standalone pages outside the main Elements interface, which allowed animated unlock buttons that confirm the tap registered at the door.

Unlock success Confirmation animation after a guest taps to unlock a door reader.
Unlock error Feedback when a pass is invalid, expired, or the reader cannot be reached.

Results & outcomes

Guest Pass released in Q4 2021. It is included in sales demonstrations and has generated ongoing requests for additional functionality.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
Two users, one feature

Designing for a system owner and a one-time visitor required two separate interfaces on a shared data model, with clear ownership of decisions for each audience.

Flows first in a complex system

Mapping the end-to-end flow before high-fidelity design surfaced the re-send problem early, when restructuring was still inexpensive.

Motion earns its keep at the door

Unlock animation gives guests immediate confirmation at the door and reduces repeated taps on the reader.

Vendor-Client Gateway

External-facing portal for CobbleStone's contract management platform. The experience was outdated, difficult to use, and underperforming with clients.

MY ROLE UX Designer, redesign lead
TIMELINE 2025 · phase 1 of 3
THE TEAM Stakeholders, engineering

Project overview

The challenge

The Vendor Client Gateway is an add-on for Contract Insights, CobbleStone's procurement and contract platform. Clients and support staff had raised concerns for years about a non-responsive layout, outdated UI patterns, and per-client customization that often reduced usability.

The solution

During a broader UX initiative across the platform, I led the Gateway redesign with responsive layouts, clearer navigation, UI patterns aligned to Contract Insights, and a narrower set of configuration options.

Where it sits

This work is phase 1 of a three-phase roadmap to redesign existing features, expand capabilities, and iterate based on live feedback.

GOAL Adoption

Increase adoption of a module clients had been avoiding by removing the friction that limited everyday use.

Responsive breakpoints Desktop + mobile
Core surfaces redesigned Home, solicitations, news
Roadmap Phase 1 of 3

What was going wrong

An audit of the existing gateway and review of comparable vendor portals identified four problems to address first.

ISSUE 01 Not responsive

Vendors working away from a desk encountered a desktop-only layout on mobile devices.

ISSUE 02 Dated patterns

Interactions that no longer matched what users expect anywhere else, which hurt both usability and trust.

ISSUE 03 Over-customization

Clients could configure the interface into states that reduced usability for their vendors.

ISSUE 04 Inconsistent workflows

Solicitation and contract management behaved differently depending on where you entered them.

Results & outcomes

Phase 1 establishes patterns that make the module easier to adopt. The next two phases will build on that foundation.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
Less configurability can be the feature

Removing configuration options was the most difficult stakeholder conversation and the most meaningful improvement. Flexibility that allows clients to degrade their vendors' experience is not worth preserving.

Audit before you redesign

Documenting what existed and why it had been added made the case for standardization more persuasive than visual inspiration alone.

A mature product moves in phases

Sequencing the work as redesign, then expand, then iterate kept scope honest and gave engineering something shippable at each step.

JRCS Command Task

Design challenge to warn a ship's bridge of an imminent collision without obscuring other critical navigation information.

MY ROLE UX Designer (solo)
TIMELINE 2023 · time-boxed challenge
REVIEWED BY JRCS Digital Innovation Lab

The brief

The problem

JRCS Digital Innovation Lab develops a command application that gives captains and officers navigation information for their vessel and highlights nearby traffic through AR. The team provided two existing screens and asked me to design a collision warning pattern.

Users

Captains and watch officers who monitor dense navigation displays for extended periods.

REQUIREMENTS
Must grab the user's attention
Must carry enough information to act on
Cannot drown out any other critical information

Sketches to shipped-looking UI

My first approach was a popover pointing at the vessel on a collision course. After testing three shapes, a rectangular window provided the necessary detail without obscuring the chart.

Figure 1.1 — The two screens provided at the start of the challenge.

1. Sketches Three popover shapes and a warning treatment for the compass-like view. I also explored projected ship paths and removed that concept because it would be difficult to implement in rough seas and computationally expensive.
2. Lo-fi wireframes Narrowing to the primary view and comparing a couple of popover structures.
3. Hi-fi progression Styled to the application's existing UI standards, then refined with clearer labels and a boat indicator.
4. Popover in context The warning sitting on the real bridge display, competing with everything else on screen.
Attention mechanism

Peripheral attention from game UI patterns

First-person shooters such as Halo and Call of Duty use edge-of-screen cues to signal urgency without blocking the view. I applied that pattern as a pulsing red bar at the top of the display. It reaches peripheral vision without covering navigation data.

Legible at sunrise, sunset, and night

A warning must remain legible across lighting conditions. I evaluated the design against sunrise, sunset, and night backgrounds and tested it with color-blind simulation filters so red was not the only signal.

Figure 3.1 — Transparency compared across sunrise, sunset, and night backgrounds.

Figure 3.2 — The warning under color-blind simulation filters.

How it ended

The team responded positively to the work, although the role ultimately went to another candidate. It remains one of the most engaging problems I have worked on.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
Feasibility is a design constraint

The projected-path concept was the strongest early sketch and the first removed from scope. Rough seas and computational cost determined that decision, not visual preference.

Borrow from outside the domain

Game interfaces have long addressed the problem of urgent but non-blocking alerts. That research direction informed the pulsing bar more than a review of competing maritime products.

Prioritize under a time box

With limited domain context, framing users and requirements up front was what made the rest of the work defensible.
